When Should You Stop Doing Your Own Small Business Tax Prep? Complexity Decides, Not Revenue

There is no revenue number that tells you to stop doing your own small business tax prep. The trigger is complexity: once you’re running payroll, collecting sales tax, carrying more than one income stream, or paying quarterly estimates, that’s usually the point to bring in a CPA, even if revenue still feels modest (FOCUS CPA, 2025).

Our instructors get asked this all the time. One founder, for example, handled her own books for four years, then hired one part-time person and started selling in a second state, and she wanted to know which number on her P&L meant it was time to get help. No number on her P&L was going to tell her. The second state would. A new set of tax rules is exactly the kind of complexity a CPA is for.

That distinction matters more in December than at any other time of year, because December is when your options are still open and April is when they aren’t.

Is there a magic revenue number for hiring a CPA?

No. FOCUS CPA’s 2025 breakdown of when to hire a CPA says it plainly: no fixed revenue figure applies to every business, and the real trigger depends on complexity rather than income alone. A consultant billing $300,000 a year from one client with no employees and no inventory may genuinely be fine with software and a careful hour each month. A caterer doing $90,000 with two 1099 contractors, equipment purchases, mileage, and sales tax on retail items has a harder return than the consultant does.

Match the help to the mess. That’s the whole calculation.

Most owners land somewhere in the middle, and the data says we’re split almost evenly on how we cope: 35% of small business owners and freelancers manage taxes through software and 33% hire an accountant, according to the FreshBooks 2025 Small Business Tax Trends Report. The same survey found only 26% feel completely confident about their taxes. Three out of four of us are filing something we’re not sure about, which is a lot of people quietly hoping.

What small business tax prep should look like in year one, year three, and year five

Starting out, through year two. Your job is boring and non-negotiable: a separate business bank account, a categorization habit you actually keep, and receipts stored somewhere other than your car. Software is usually enough here. The reason to care this early isn’t the return; it’s cash. You can’t manage cash you haven’t recorded, and the early years are unforgiving: federal data shows about 21% of new businesses close within their first year (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics). The books won’t save a business with no market, but they will tell you which one you have in time to do something about it.

Years three to five. This is where complexity arrives without announcing itself. A contractor here, a retainer there, a second product line, a state you didn’t expect. Of businesses that opened in 2021, about 53% had closed within five years (BLS). And for a lot of us, the main accounting system is still a founder’s memory of what that charge was for.

This is the stage to stop asking can I do this and start asking should I still be the one doing this. Quinta’s Bookkeeping II class is built for exactly that handoff: a clean set of books so your bookkeeper isn’t billing you hourly to untangle a shoebox.

Year five and beyond, with growth. Payroll changes the stakes. Payroll tax is held in trust, the deadlines are rigid, and nobody grades on effort. By year ten, about two-thirds of businesses have closed (of those that opened in 2016, about 34% were still open ten years later, per the BLS), and those rates have held fairly steady across years of data, which is oddly comforting. Stable numbers mean the failure modes are known. Known failure modes can be prepared for.

What bad bookkeeping actually costs, in dollars and IRS code

Vague warnings about “staying organized” don’t move anyone. Specific penalties do.

The accuracy-related penalty is 20% of the underpayment when it comes from negligence, no reasonable attempt to follow the rules, or a substantial understatement of income tax (Internal Revenue Code §6662). A return filed more than 60 days late owes at least $525, or the full tax due if that’s less, for returns due after December 31, 2025 (IRS).

Then there’s the quieter cost: overpaying. Money you sent in voluntarily because your books didn’t show the deduction you’d earned. No letter arrives. No penalty notice. You just paid for the privilege of disorganized records.

Doing it yourself to save $200 a month stops looking thrifty the first time a missed deduction or a late payroll deposit costs more than a year of help.

Why you still prep for tax season yourself, even with a bookkeeper and a CPA

Because the time doesn’t actually transfer. The NSBA 2025 Small Business Taxation Survey found that most small-business owners spend more than 20 hours a year on federal taxes even though the vast majority already pay an outside tax practitioner. Ninety percent say federal taxes affect their day-to-day operations, with one in three calling the impact significant. Hiring help changes what those hours are made of; it doesn’t delete them.

The IRS agrees, in its own paperwork: it estimates about 21 hours, on average, for individual filers with a business, and 40 to 50 hours for small partnership, corporation and S-corp returns, record-keeping included.

Your CPA can file the return. Only you know whether the March hardware store charge was a client job or a shelf for your garage.

And the cost of leaving it all to April is not only financial. Xero’s Emotional Tax Return 2026 report found owners give up an average of 22 hours a month to financial management, and nearly half have hidden business-related financial stress from a partner or family member. That last figure is the one I’d underline. The hours are recoverable. The habit of carrying it alone is harder to undo.
