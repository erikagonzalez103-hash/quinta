What Do AI Tools Cost for a Small Business? And Three Subscriptions Worth Cancelling First

What AI tools cost for a small business runs from zero to five figures, and most of us land near the bottom: the median small business spends $2,200 a year on AI, with 42% spending $1,000 or less annually (SBE Council, Small Business Technology Use Survey, March 2026), and a third of small business owners spend nothing at all (Bluevine, 2026 Small Business AI Trends Report). If you sat down with your card statement this afternoon, there's a good chance you'd cancel two or three AI subscriptions, and the work wouldn't notice.

Bluevine's spending bands show how small that gap usually is. 16% of small businesses spend $100 to $249 a month on AI; 28% spend $25 to $99. For a lot of owners, the difference between those two bands is a couple of tools they stopped opening months ago.

What the average small business spends on AI tools per year

The spread here is wider than any average suggests. SBE Council (March 2026) found a median annual spend of $2,200 and a median of five AI tools per business, but 8% of respondents spend more than $25,000 a year. Bluevine (2026) found only 10% of small businesses spend $250 or more a month, and that free tiers are likely carrying part of current adoption.

So two owners can both say “we use AI” and mean a $0 chat window and a $30,000 stack.

The subscription line isn't the whole bill, either. Digital Applied (February 2026) puts the average small business at roughly $2,400 a year in AI subscriptions, and estimates that once you add training time, workflow disruption and integration maintenance, a team of ten to twenty is really spending $4,000 to $5,000 a year. Its other warning is the one worth keeping: three lightly integrated tools are manageable; ten become a full-time maintenance burden. Nobody budgets for the Tuesday an API changes.

Three AI subscriptions worth cancelling first

The duplicate. Two tools doing the same job, and you keep paying for the one you stopped opening because cancelling feels like admitting you chose wrong. Lilach Bullock's write-up of her own subscription audit describes a client, Marcus, who runs a four-person bookkeeping firm in Leeds: eleven AI subscriptions, including three transcription tools that all did the same thing and two “AI content assistants” neither he nor his office manager had logged into in four months. Bullock's own audit took her from sixteen tools to seven, and from £612 a month to £314.

The writing assistant you rewrite every time. If a tool hands you a draft you have to repair, it isn't saving you the drafting. It's charging you to argue with a stranger about your own sentences.

The add-on that's good but redundant. A meeting-notes feature can be genuinely useful and still duplicate something already in your stack. That's the one that stings, because the problem isn't quality. Whitebeard Strategies (2026) names it precisely: the hidden cost of a new tool isn't the subscription, it's the context switching, the learning curve, and the abandoned workflows left behind.

What a lean stack can look like: one general assistant for drafting and thinking out loud (for many of us, that's Claude), one automation tool, one scheduling tool, one transcription tool. Four. Whitebeard (2026) finds most businesses getting strong returns from AI running three to seven tools, with two or three of those core to daily operations. Running seven and treating all of them as core is how you end up with seven.

Why a bakery's AI stack should not look like a consultant's

This is the part the spending averages flatten. Homebase's 2026 Main Street AI Gap Report surveyed 750 small business decision-makers and found 74% now using or piloting AI, but adoption runs 54% among businesses under $500K in revenue and 81% among businesses at $5M and up. By sector, computer hardware and software businesses sit at 94%; food service and restaurants at 65%.

Read that as a map, not a scoreboard. A bakery owner's bottleneck is dough at 4am, staffing, and a POS that doesn't talk to her scheduling. A solo consultant's bottleneck is proposals, follow-up and notes. The consultant should probably be paying for more AI than the baker, and the baker should not feel one ounce of guilt about it.

For solo and service businesses, the upside is real. MBO Partners' State of Independence 2026 found nearly eight in ten independent professionals say AI complements their work, and generative AI users report saving nearly 10 hours a week. That's most of a workday returned to a person with no team to delegate to. There's a catch on the other side of the ledger: Upwork's Future Workforce Index 2026 found per-contract earnings for generative AI and creative production work down 13%, even as the number of those contracts rose. The savings land in your calendar, and sometimes the market takes a slice back out of your rate. Worth knowing before you price your next project.

If you want a number to budget against, SUCCESS (2026) suggests starting at 1% to 3% of revenue for all technology, then carving 20% to 30% of that for AI experimentation. For many small businesses, that math produces a monthly figure smaller than a phone bill, and it's a relief to see it written down.

How to run your own AI subscription audit in one afternoon

SUCCESS (2026) gives the formula worth using: Total First-Year Cost = (Monthly Subscription × 12) + (Setup Hours × Hourly Rate) + (Training Hours × Hourly Rate) + (Lost Productivity Value). Run it per tool, against what the tool actually produced, not what the sales page said it would.

Then do the colder pass. Open your card statement. List every AI charge. Next to each one, write the last task it did for you by name. If you can't name the task, you've found your cancellation.

One caution on the evidence people cite at you: Intuit QuickBooks' 2026 AI Impact Report found roughly 8 in 10 businesses that paid for AI in 2024 were still paying in 2025, alongside 78% of surveyed US businesses reporting improved productivity and 43% reporting increased revenue. The productivity and revenue findings are meaningful. Retention is a weaker signal than it looks; it's also what every unused gym membership looks like in a dataset.

And feelings count as data. If a tool is technically earning its keep and you dread opening it, that's information about fit, not a flaw in you.

What AI tools cost for a small business is a question you can answer in an afternoon with a statement and a pen, and the answer is almost always lower than the internet implies.
