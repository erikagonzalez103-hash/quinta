What Does “Failing Forward” Mean in Business? And How to Tell a Stall From an Exit

Failing forward in business means treating a setback as information you act on inside a set timeframe, rather than a verdict on whether you belong in the work. John C. Maxwell’s line, “Fail early, fail often, but always fail forward,” gives you the posture. It doesn’t give you the method. The method is figuring out which kind of failure you’re actually holding: an ordinary stall, a fixable internal problem, or a business that has already told you the truth and is waiting for you to answer it.

The phrase has been printed on enough mugs to lose its teeth. What the mug leaves out is that sometimes forward points out the door, and that leaving on purpose is a different act than leaving because you ran out of road.

Everyone says “fail forward.” You’ve probably seen it on a coffee mug somewhere. But no one actually tells you how.

Failing forward isn’t a feeling you talk yourself into. It’s one honest question: is this a stall, or is it time to change how you’re running things?

Most of the time it’s a stall, and most stalls are boring and completely fixable. Around year three, the decisions that got you started stop working. Maybe you’re still charging what you charged when you were brand new, even though you’re a lot better at this now. Or every single thing still has to go through you, so the business can only grow as fast as you can personally work.

The good news is that both of those are things you can actually fix. Start small. Pick one task you do over and over and hand it off this month.

And before you even think about walking away, try pruning. Look at who you’re actually serving, and narrow it down to the clients who are the best fit and pay what you’re worth. That one change made a huge difference for me. Then look at how you work. I started using AI to get through the busywork faster, and that’s what finally let me change how I price.

Is a business plateau normal at year three? Yes, and it’s a common place to hit one

Year three is when the early shortcuts come due. Small Business Resources names the usual suspects: founder fatigue, accumulated process debt, shifting market fit and team misalignment, rather than bad luck. One study of 100 early-stage companies, published by Harvard Business Review, found about 70% started running into morale and culture problems in years three and four. If you built the thing yourself, year three is roughly when everything you duct-taped in year one starts billing you interest.

The survival curve says the same thing more bluntly. Bureau of Labor Statistics data, as analyzed by Statistics Fundamentals (2026), shows roughly one in five businesses closes in year one, about half by year five, and two-thirds by year ten. Most owners who have been at it long enough to have returning clients are standing on that terrain. A flat year is ordinary, and it doesn’t require an explanation.

One more number. A 2026 survey of 1,000 U.S. business leaders by the coaching platform Scaling.com, reported by Business Journal Daily, found 59% admit they’ve mistaken being busy for making progress, with leaders naming resource limitations (58%), economic conditions (54%) and operational constraints (48%) as the top drivers of stagnation. A stall can run eighteen months while your calendar stays completely full, and a full calendar is the one piece of evidence most of us accept without checking it.

Most growth stalls come from inside the company, which is good news and also a to-do list

A 2008 Harvard Business Review study of large-company growth stalls, by Matthew Olson, Derek van Bever and Seth Verry, found that external factors like regulation and downturns explained only about 13% of stalls. The rest traced to choices within management’s control. That’s a plateau you can diagnose and rehab, which is better than a plateau you can only endure.

Diagnosis is where right-fit thinking matters, because a stall in a two-person service studio and a stall in a product company carrying inventory don’t have the same cause or the same fix. CB Insights’ 2024 analysis of 431 failed venture-backed startups, cited in The Next Scoop’s 2026 analysis, found poor product-market fit behind about 43% of them, with running out of cash showing up as the symptom that gets blamed. For a product business, that points at the offer itself. For a service business billing hours, the usual culprit is simpler and more boring: the price was set three years ago, for a version of you who worked faster and cheaper. That’s a math problem, and it’s fixable in an afternoon.

The other common culprit is a founder whose name sits on every line. That shows up not just in the calendar but in the year-end financials, and it doesn’t come down to how hard anyone is working.

If that’s the shape of your stall, and the advice you keep getting is “scale,” the first move is smaller than that: one repeatable task leaving your hands. A single task, not a reorg.

How to know when to hold and when to actually use your exit strategy

Annie Duke’s Quit (2022) makes the case that most people quit too late rather than too early, because we overvalue grit and underprice the cost of staying in the wrong thing. Her practical tool is kill criteria: a state plus a date, written down at the beginning. If I haven’t hit X by March, I stop. Decided while you’re calm, not at 11pm in a spreadsheet.

Her second rule is the close-call rule, and it will annoy you. If the decision between staying and going feels genuinely close, quitting is usually the better call. Grit is a fine trait and an expensive strategy. This was not my strategy, and I honestly don’t know if that was a good or bad thing. We are still in business, but it cost us.

Redirecting a failure often beats closing the doors, and kill criteria also work at smaller levels. Think in terms of killing an offer, a service line, a location or a client segment, not only the whole company. You can retire the workshop that never sold and keep the entity, the EIN, the bank account and the eight years of reputation attached to your name. Most of what founders call “quitting” is really pruning that nobody gave them permission to do. For example, a friend and colleague recently reimagined her law firm, cutting key service offerings that were quickly eating away her profits and her time. The exit strategy stays in the drawer; the thing that was draining you goes.

Write both versions down before you need them: the best case, the worst case, and your honest read on the odds. A decision made in advance is the only kind that doesn’t cost you a month.

Women founders’ confidence is already accurate. The missing input is usually capital or counsel

Research by Jennifer Jennings and colleagues at the University of Alberta, published in Entrepreneurship Theory and Practice (2023), found women are as likely as men to hold accurate entrepreneurial confidence: about 70% of women accurately assessed their own entrepreneurial ability, versus roughly 64% of men, with men showing slightly higher rates of overconfidence.

The evidence isn’t unanimous. A 2026 study of Finland’s 2025 Global Entrepreneurship Monitor survey (2,050 adults) found women markedly less likely than men to report moderate or strong entrepreneurial self-efficacy, and noted that owning a business strengthens that self-belief, which men do more often. Big caveat: self-efficacy on a survey measures how many times you’ve been in the room. It doesn’t measure the quality of your judgment.

Which is why “rebuild your confidence” is bad advice at a plateau and “get another set of eyes on the numbers” is good advice. The Federal Reserve’s 2026 Report on Employer Firms, drawn from its 2025 Small Business Credit Survey, found 77% of small employer firms cited rising costs or tariff-related costs as a financial challenge, and only 42% of applicants received the full financing amount they sought. Fewer than half got funded in full. A woman who pauses before her year-three decision is holding an accurate read of a market where the capital she modeled may not arrive, and what she’s missing is the funding or a second set of eyes on the plan.

AmPac’s report on the year-three milestone also cites SCORE data that I’ve talked about before: mentored businesses were 12% more likely than the national average to still be in business after one year, and 43% of owners with five or more mentoring sessions reported measurable growth, versus 30% after a single session. Mentoring moves the survival number by a measurable amount.

Start with the one number you’ve been avoiding

Failing forward in business is something you schedule (or expect) rather than something you summon after a bad quarter. If you are anything like me, these have been some of the biggest lessons of the journey. Pick the decision you’ve been carrying for three months. Write the kill criteria: a state and a date. Then find one person who has made that call before and ask them what they’d want to know.
